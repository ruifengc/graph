# Editorial composition

Use this when shaping a page, not as a fixed page skeleton.

Start with the reader's task: understand a mechanism, weigh findings,
compare alternatives, follow a development, or learn how to do something.
Lead with an answer or orientation that the evidence supports. A neutral
question or a statement of uncertainty may be more accurate than a verdict.

Useful structures include:

- Question → mechanism → worked example → implications and limits.
- Finding → evidence → experimental conditions → what remains unknown.
- Shared question → competing accounts → points of agreement/disagreement.
- Overview → stages or concepts → optional detail and source references.

These are examples. Choose the sequence for the subject, combine where
helpful, and let content determine length and section size.

## What deserves space

A visual earns its space by making a relationship, change, comparison,
mechanism, or quantity easier to understand. An annotated source figure,
short equation, or table may work better than a custom chart. Prose may
carry an explanation without a diagram. Avoid repeating a graphic's
entire content in text; explain the implication or needed context.

Use informative headings, readable labels, and local caveats. Provide an
overview and a discoverable route through the explanation. Necessary steps
may unfold through interaction; offer a readable alternative to timed
playback and keep essential information accessible beyond hover. For long
material, provide orientation and navigation rather than stretching a
summary over empty screens.

## Depth and information density

Density is the amount of connected understanding a reader gets from the
space and effort spent. Neither crowded text nor a sparse sequence of
headline cards guarantees it.

Preserve the material that answers the next important question: how the
mechanism works, what a concrete example does, why the comparison is fair,
and where the conclusion stops holding. A source with substantial reasoning
deserves more than its headline results. A shorter source may support a
compact page; missing evidence should remain a gap.

Let text, visual form, and any operation share the explanatory work. For
example, a process can expose inputs, intermediate states, and outputs
beside their explanation; unrelated summary boxes do not show that process.
Use details and local source access to keep depth available without placing
everything on the first screen. Avoid repeating the same claim as a title,
a large number, a chart, and a takeaway without adding understanding.

## Scope and attribution

Preserve findings and perspectives that materially affect understanding.
Do not promise to replace the whole source when the page covers selected
parts. Attribute opinions and distinguish reported findings from your
interpretation. Link important claims to specific source locations so the
reader can inspect the underlying evidence.
