# Visual explainer creation

Turn the material into an original webpage that is worth reading and exploring. Understand its content, genre, and audience before choosing an organization: a paper may need its mechanism explained; an interview may need to preserve disagreements; a personal account may center on specific experiences, observations, and judgments. Make clear relationships explicit. When the material covers several topics, explain them separately if appropriate; do not force them into a single theory or causal chain.

Write clearly in language suited to the material. Preserve the author's meaningful observations, judgments, hesitation, and distinctive expression while consolidating spoken repetition. Do not inflate ordinary observations into grand conclusions, repeatedly distill an "essence," force symmetrical phrasing, or impose unsupported frameworks for the sake of neatness. Choose natural, specific titles and prose grounded in the content. Distinguish your summaries and explanations from the author's intended meaning. Do not put polished wording in quotation marks or present it as the author's words without a reliable original. Acknowledge ambiguities in transcription.

Identify the relationships, processes, and comparisons readers need to understand. Explain them through a combination of text, spatial organization, graphics, and operations that add explanatory value. For material with a clear mechanism or argument, explain how it works, why it is designed that way, and its effects or trade-offs, using examples and conditions supported by the material. Distinguish your own reasoning from source claims; do not fill gaps with unsupported details. Let rich material retain its depth. Give adjacent content a clear relationship, and avoid repetitive summaries and meaningless decoration.

Create a clear reading path that brings each question, explanation, relevant data, and illustration together as a recognizable whole. Place details side by side when comparison helps, and unfold them step by step when sequence aids understanding. Use a small, consistent set of type sizes for body text, headings, supporting information, and data; use spacing, alignment, and contrast to reinforce hierarchy, and keep supporting text readable. Visuals and explanations should serve the content without having to reproduce the source's presentation. Let the material determine the structure, palette, technology, and number of interactions. When interactive reading is appropriate, provide light and dark themes, ensuring text, charts, and states remain clear in both.

Choose interactions for their explanatory value. When differences between approaches or conditions are key to understanding, prefer a shared reference around the same object: let readers switch approaches or adjust important conditions and see the graphics, values, and explanations change together. Do not add controls when a static comparison is sufficiently clear. Complete the chosen interactions: make entry points discoverable, show the current state, keep graphics, values, and explanations consistent, and allow readers to revisit important states. Make static explanations clear and effective too. Complete and check one coherent path through reading and operation before expanding the content; do not leave graphics or controls that appear usable but are unimplemented.

Distinguish observations in the material, author claims, your own explanations, and teaching assumptions. Do not present a teaching illustration as an empirical measurement. Check numbers, units, comparison baselines, and derivations that affect conclusions. Preserve uncertainty when the material cannot support a judgment, and make important content traceable to the relevant source material.

Review the visuals and behavior as a finished webpage: reading flows smoothly, the visual expression has a distinct identity suited to the subject, the page remains understandable on narrow screens, and operations provide stable feedback.

When further guidance is useful, consult the relevant material-handling notes in `workflows/` and the narrative, interaction, and chart notes in `references/`. These offer considerations, not a prescribed layout. The checking and preview tools in `scripts/` can assist verification but do not replace review of the content and actual interactions.
